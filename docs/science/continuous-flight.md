# Continuous Earth-to-space camera

There is one authoritative `Observer`, one eye position and one optical lens from ground level through space. Opening an information or orbit-annotation panel does not park, rescale or replace that observer. The display may change its internal floating origin or visible detail, but those presentation changes cannot move the physical eye. Explicit **Fit Earth**, **Fit whole orbit**, coordinate entry and ground actions are deliberate navigation commands; a fit may fail if the current narrow lens would require a position outside the supported domain.

## Coordinates and finite domain

The existing WGS84 geodetic feet position, effective eye offset and full heading/pitch/roll triad remain stored in `f64`. The eye is converted to ECEF before astronomical sampling, measurement and display. The supported ellipsoidal-height interval is **−10,000 m through four astronomical units**, where `1 au = 149597870700 m` exactly and the upper endpoint is `598391482800 m`. The astronomical unit and exact light speed are listed in [JPL's astrodynamic parameters](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/astro_par.html). Four AU is an application limit, not an astronomical boundary or a precision guarantee.

Explicit geodetic input remains strictly bounded. An inverse Cartesian conversion accepts endpoint roundoff of `max(0.00001 m, 16 ε |P|)`, with IEEE binary64 epsilon `ε`; only this numerical endpoint allowance is clamped. Its maximum is about 2.13 mm in this domain. Independently generated high-precision Cartesian fixtures test the larger range; existing near-Earth tests keep their original tighter scale. Printed decimal digits and small numerical residuals do not imply equivalent real-world accuracy.

With horizon lock inactive, the full camera axes are transported across changes in the local ENU basis. Mouse yaw rotates the triad about its current camera up axis, then pitch about the yawed right axis, using Rodrigues' rotation formula. `set_view_axes` stores the resulting orientation as heading, pitch and roll. Unrestricted passage through zenith or nadir therefore does not clamp the physical sight direction or flip the camera. Active [horizon lock](horizon-lock.md) subsequently chooses local zero bank, whose upright frame has a declared vertical-view singularity. These are free-flight controls, not a constrained upright walking simulation.

When [horizon lock](horizon-lock.md) is inactive, Q/E add left/right body roll around the current forward axis. For signed right-roll input `r` and elapsed time `dt`, the applied angle is `α = clamp(r,−1,1) × (90°/s) × min(dt,0.25 s)`. Nonfinite input, nonpositive time and zero input leave the pose unchanged. With orthonormal forward `F` and camera-up `U_body`, Rodrigues' formula reduces to `U_body' = U_body cos α + (F × U_body) sin α`; forward stays fixed and the right axis is `F × U_body'`. Positive E input leans camera-up toward camera-right. This fixed rate is a usability choice and does not depend on lens zoom. Roll changes only the view triad: the configured eye offset is still measured from the feet along geodetic up, not along the rolled camera axis. The default horizon lock sets bank to zero through 100 km WGS84 ellipsoidal eye height while preserving heading, pitch and position. This is a practical control threshold, not a physical atmosphere boundary; outside it or with the setting off, full-triad roll remains unrestricted.

## Responsive inspection motion

The normalized input direction combines camera forward, camera right and camera up: `d = normalize(iF F + iR R + iU U_body)`. W/S select forward/back, A/D select left/right, and Space/Shift select positive/negative body-up. Opposing keys cancel. Pitch and bank therefore affect ascending/descending controls, and diagonal input cannot multiply the requested speed. Releasing capture or movement stops translation immediately; no artificial inertia is accumulated. The wheel changes a stored base speed, while telescope wheel input changes the existing lens instead.

With adaptive speed enabled, define clearance `c` as the nonnegative feet height above the active modeled ground, further limited by eye distance to the displayed apparent Sun and Moon spheres. Then:

```text
v = min(10^11 m/s, base_speed × (1 + c/50 m) × precision)
precision = 0.1 with Ctrl, otherwise 1
```

Disabling adaptation replaces `(1+c/50 m)` by one. The 50 m scale, multipliers and speed limit are declared usability choices. These potentially superluminal camera speeds do **not** represent a spacecraft trajectory, acceleration, energy, proper time or aberration velocity.

The scientific compatibility method `FlightController::step` retains geodetic-up input and its optional factor-eight boost for existing callers and fixtures. Interactive controls use `step_body` with boost disabled; both methods share the same integrator and collision policy. The continuous native diagnostic expresses geodetic vertical as its dot products with the three camera axes, then calls `step_body`. Thus its six-second outward/return route remains comparable without resetting camera orientation or bypassing the new controller.

G queries the active canonical ground at the feet's latitude/longitude and lands upright and level. With local east/north basis `E,N`, heading is `atan2(F·E,F·N)` when the horizontal projection exceeds `64 ε`; otherwise the stored heading is retained. Pitch and roll become zero, and the existing ground-action base speed becomes 10 m/s. The pole convention remains the chosen local ENU longitude. Upright is the geodetic normal rather than a sloping triangle normal. Eye/posture settings remain unchanged. Ground placement and orientation are prepared on a candidate observer and committed together; a failed ground or orientation query leaves the original pose intact. G's input frame ends immediately after landing, so held roll or movement cannot undo the reset in that frame.

The clearance law is evaluated during movement, rather than multiplying the initial speed by a whole frame duration. A scalar path-length ODE along the frame's normalized ECEF direction uses fourth-order Runge–Kutta with step doubling. The local error estimate is `|two_half_steps − one_full_step|/15`; the accepted tolerance is the maximum of 0.1 mm, `10^-7` times the proposed increment and the coordinate roundoff allowance. A control update processes at most 0.25 s and 128 trials. Exhausted work stops the unchecked remainder and reports blockage. This finite numerical contract is not exact frame-rate invariance for arbitrary changing input, terrain or floating-point values.

Each accepted segment checks the active canonical Earth faces/reference ellipsoid and the current apparent celestial sphere packet. The celestial stop is a display/navigation constraint at a frozen sampled epoch during that update, not a simultaneous physical-surface collision or a continuously resampled moving-body collision. The final feet must remain above the active ground and below the reserved standing-eye ceiling. A separate conservative guard prevents a fast step from passing entirely below a raised finite patch through its open coverage edges when neither endpoint lies in that patch. Let `s` be the minimum ellipsoid-scaled norm along the accepted segment. A lower height bound is `b(s−1)` outside the ellipsoid or `a(s−1)` inside it. Expanding each patch's Cartesian vertex bounds by its maximum positive height minus that lower bound encloses potentially hidden subsurface portions. An uncertain crossing is refused; this may also reject a harmless near-edge segment. Ascending or taking a different path can resolve it. This guard and endpoint/face checks are not a universal swept-volume certificate for arbitrary nonconvex coverage or cases with an endpoint already in the patch. No vertical walls or blending elevations are invented.

Celestial measurement and movement use the same stable sphere roots, but interpret an existing contact differently. A range query still reports a zero-distance contact or the forward exit from inside a sphere. Movement must permit escape. For each sphere separately, let `o = eye − center`, `m = o·direction` for the unit movement direction, and `δ` be the maximum of the eye/center coordinate allowances and `16 ε radius`. When `|o| <= radius + δ`, movement refuses `m < −δ`; otherwise it permits outward or tangent escape. Along the frozen straight-eye proxy, `r(s)^2 = |o|^2 + 2ms + s^2`, so any permitted initial radial decrease is at most `δ`. Outside that band, the nearest entry or tangent contact still clips the step. Escaping one body does not skip checks against the other. This is an eye-point navigation policy, not body-volume collision: the actual eye also follows the changing geodetic-up posture offset above the linear feet segment, so its path is slightly curved. No exact swept-eye-path claim is made.

## Time, reference frames and apparent astronomy

Normal following keeps the eye fixed to Earth between navigation edits. F9 instead holds the eye and camera in Earth-centered nonrotating celestial axes (active horizon lock can still replace camera bank with local upright); the origin still translates with Earth's center. Neither mode becomes a heliocentric spacecraft trajectory. Time playback, reversal and date changes continue through the same [tracking and collision policy](reference-frames.md), without a parked overview exception.

The light-time solver uses the actual distant reception position and the existing DE440s ephemeris. Runtime `sample_for_inspection` uses a **bounded surface-observatory receiver reference** for aberration. Convert the actual eye to geodetic latitude/longitude, place the reference at WGS84 `h=0` at those same angles, and form:

```text
v_receiver = v_Earth,barycentric + C(t)^T (omega × P_reference)   # following Earth
v_receiver = v_Earth,barycentric                                # F9 unlocked
```

The reference is the mathematical ellipsoid, not terrain, geoid or a physical observatory asset. Its spin contribution is at most approximately 465.1 m/s and changes continuously with latitude/longitude; it does not grow with flight height. At smooth ground it preserves diurnal aberration apart from the tiny eye-height difference. Actual eye position still determines all parallax and reception light times. The **I** panel names this convention. It is not the derivative of the virtual camera trajectory; neither camera flight speed nor an artificial astronomical-distance co-rotation speed is used as an observer velocity.

This one convention applies at all heights, without an altitude switch. The original scientific `sample_with_tracking` API retains actual-eye co-rotation for reference tests, separately from application inspection sampling. Both methods validate subluminal finite velocities. [NAIF's aberration reference](https://naif.jpl.nasa.gov/pub/naif/toolkit_docs/C/req/abcorr.html) distinguishes receive-time position from observer-velocity corrections; this application uses its separately documented relativistic center-direction formula.

The Earth surface remains instantaneous geometric ECEF. Sun/Moon are adopted spheres with retarded centers and the declared center-direction aberration correction. This is **not** a complete retarded image of Earth's moving terrain, a per-limb light-time solution, a Lorentz-transformed extended-body image, a physical spacecraft camera or gravitational light bending. The bounded receiver convention avoids the gross inconsistency that arose when artificial AU-scale co-rotation aberrated Sun/Moon against an untransformed Earth image. Remaining approximation and source uncertainty are explicit; the application does not claim a universally exact observed solar-system photograph. Calendar and EOP/model-quality limits are unchanged.

The observer eclipse readout uses independently converged Sun/Moon emission states at that actual eye. The local surface eclipse approximation is not extended across AU: beyond an eye radius of one billion metres it is independently solved at Earth's center, and its spatial offset remains limited to 1.1 billion metres. This keeps Earth illumination separate from the distant-eye local field. The annual path and CIP/plane strokes are simultaneous geometric annotations, explicitly distinct from the apparent displayed Sun/Moon centers.
